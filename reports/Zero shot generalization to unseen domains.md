# LIDM learned to read labels, not intents

After three training stages, LIDM's two-pass design works as built, but its main premise has not held up. **No LIDM configuration beats a free 110M bge-base bi-encoder on zero-shot Banking77.** bge scores 0.695 at N=77 with label names and 0.732 with `name: description` options, while the best trained LIDM head scores 0.456 alone, or 0.505 with descriptions. The architecture does deliver things single-sequence Laya cannot: 12.6 ms per 77-option question with cached options (Laya: 17.6 ms), raw ECE of 0.03-0.10 on six of seven tasks (Laya: 0.13-0.33), and, after stage 1b's 961k distinct labels, real label reading that beats retrieval by about 20 points on held-out labels from its training domains. But it beats shipped Laya only at N=77 (0.434 vs 0.360). There it still trails fine-tuned Laya and Laya with a bge shortlist, and it loses to Laya at every smaller N and on BoolQ. The experiments and the literature give the same reason. On an unseen domain, label meaning comes from broad pretraining and from the type and variety of training labels: embedding models train on 270M-788M text pairs, label-reading classifiers on 50k-1.2M examples. Architecture moves only 1-4 points, and a head trained on top of a strong retrieval score learns corrections for specific training labels rather than a matching skill that transfers. For a 150M-400M open model on Banking77 at N=77, the realistic range is **0.70-0.72 with names** and **0.73-0.77 with contrastive descriptions**. **0.75-0.79** would match GPT-4o and Jev's full-test score, which no open small model has published. **0.85 or more needs 5-10 real examples per intent** or a frontier-LLM reranker. Zero-shot ingredients together plausibly close 5-10 of the roughly 24 points between the zero-shot floor and full supervision, while the first handful of labelled examples alone is worth 15-20.

## LIDM delivered cheap, calibrated scoring but missed every accuracy target

LIDM replaces Laya's single packed sequence with two encoder passes. Pass A encodes the state and question once per request. Pass B encodes each distinct option as its own `[CLS] [MASK] option [SEP]` sequence, so the encodings of a fixed label set can be cached. A 2-block, 32.9M-parameter fusion head lets option tokens attend to each other and to pass-A tokens, and a learned `dual_score` term adds a retrieval cosine to each logit ([ARCHITECTURE.md](../docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)). This removes Laya's structural bottleneck, where 77 options share a ~192-token head budget at about 3-4 tokens each ([GOALS.md](../docs/GOALS.md)). The mechanics work. With LoRA merged and options cached, LIDM answers a 77-option Banking77 question in **12.6 ms p50**, faster than shipped Laya's 17.6 ms. It trains in **5.5 GB of VRAM**, and it is well calibrated out of the box, with **ECE 0.03-0.10 on six of seven tasks** before any temperature fitting ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). The design doc had predicted Laya-like over-confidence, so this is the one prediction that was wrong in LIDM's favour.

On accuracy, no criterion is met. No configuration reaches Banking77 ≥ 0.70 at N=77 and ≥ 0.85 at N=20 zero-shot. None reaches BoolQ ≥ 0.826 and SST-5 ≥ 0.570 either, although LIDM's BoolQ and SST-5 runs are zero-shot while fine-tuned Laya's are in-distribution. Fresh-label latency misses its target by 0.9 ms (35.9 ms), and two-thirds of that time goes to encoding the 77 options. The calibration criterion requires per-type temperature fitting, which was never built ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md); [ARCHITECTURE.md](../docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)).

Against Laya, the record is narrow. L1 leads shipped Laya by 7.4 points at N=77 but trails it by 3-10 points at every N ≤ 40. Fine-tuned Laya beats L1 by 7-13 points at every N. Laya's cheap bge-shortlist workaround (B1b) beats L1 at N=77 by 18 points and is also faster with fresh labels. The claim that LIDM's accuracy decays more slowly with option count holds only against shipped Laya: L1 keeps 53% of its N=5 accuracy at N=77, fine-tuned Laya 56% (computed from [RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). The kill criterion has two clauses: Laya wins on nuance tasks, and label sets are typically under 20 options. The first holds. The second is a fact about deployment that no experiment measured ([GOALS.md](../docs/GOALS.md)).

The cost of the encoder split itself was never isolated, because control F, a joint encoder trained on the same data with LoRA, was never built. The design's precedents predicted 1-2 points ([DeFormer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.00697); [MORES](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.13313)). The observed 5-13-point gap to Laya mixes that cost with different training data. Three design assumptions failed early. The frozen encoder could not read unseen labels: it scored 0.044 and answered 69% of unseen-gold questions with a training label, so LoRA was needed from stage 1. Raw ModernBERT layers, whose norms grow from about 30 to about 1,200, could not be mixed until each layer was LayerNormed. Looping and bigger heads added nothing, with 0.437, 0.439 and 0.437 at 1, 2 and 4 loops ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)).

## Every LIDM head lost to its own retrieval prior on banking intents

Three diagnoses explain the table, and they form a chain. First, **low label variety leads to memorisation**. On the M0 mixture's 152 labels, M1 reached 0.76 validation accuracy while Banking77 stayed at 0.02-0.05. On 1,000 items it predicted only 26 distinct labels, with 42% of answers on one of them ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). Second, **a strong prior plus moderate variety produces explaining-away**. gte-1 trained its head next to gte's cosine from step 0. The head scored 0.689 on seen labels and 0.015 on Banking77, and 49% of its score variance was a fixed per-label offset. In RESULTS.md's words, the generalisable part of the task "is already explained, and the cheapest remaining loss reduction is per-label residuals" ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). Third, **high variety produces real reading, but only of label types the model trained on**. Stage 1b's 961k labels took the head from 0.015 to 0.456 and cut the per-label offset share to 0.22. On held-out labels from the training domains, gte-1b beats retrieval **0.905 to about 0.71**, and still by 11 points with texts cut to 10 words. It gains 12 points over gte on patent topics. It loses 16 points on Banking77 validation and 20 on consumer finance. The training labels were mostly topic categories and statements about web documents, and RESULTS.md notes that retrieval's pretraining on query-intent pairs "is far broader than our data" ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)).

The retrieval weight learned in training is also systematically wrong for unseen labels. Training learns it on seen labels, where the head beats retrieval, so it gives retrieval too little weight elsewhere. The learned weights were 7.07 vs 160 re-fitted for L1+bge, 26.6 vs 160 for gte-1, and 8.3 vs 20 for gte-1b ([ARCHITECTURE.md](../docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)). Even with the weight re-fitted, adding a confident but partly wrong head pulled gte-1b's combined score (0.599) below gte alone (0.634).

The gte-1b ablations rule out the obvious alternatives. The gap is not reasoning: the head trails retrieval most where the message shares a content word with the gold label (0.514 vs 0.722) and least on implicit messages (0.304 vs 0.405). That points to weak lexical-semantic matching of intent phrases, which is exactly what retrieval pretraining supplies. It is not hard distractors, because with nine nearest-neighbour distractors both scorers lose about 19 points. It is not instruction wording, since seven wordings move the head by only ±3 points. It is not head capacity or label format either ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). Descriptions written from label names alone lift bge from 0.696 to **0.732** and gte from 0.634 to 0.703, but they move LIDM heads inconsistently, from −5 to +8 points depending on format. No combination beats the best plain bi-encoder. gte-1's nominal 0.7065 with descriptions comes from a head that scores 0.022 on its own ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md); [eval-b77-descriptions.log](../logs/eval-b77-descriptions.log)).

One number leaves room for a head. Next to bge, L1's head was right on 4.8% of questions where bge was wrong, so a perfect per-question chooser would score **0.743**. But disagreements favoured bge 713 to 155, and no fixed weight captured that 4.8% ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). The pattern is consistent: LIDM's head reads labels that look like its training labels well, and it complements retrieval strongly on seen labels (+21 points combined for gte-1). It reads customer intents worse than a model that never saw a classification task. All runs are single-seed, and on 3,080 items a 95% binomial interval is about ±1.6 points at 70% accuracy (my calculation), so gaps of 1-2 points are noise.

## Pretraining breadth and label diversity carry transfer to unseen labels

The literature explains why a bi-encoder with no task training sets the bar. **Retrieval encoders see two to four orders of magnitude more matching data than label-reading classifiers.** E5 trains on about 270M consistency-filtered pairs, filtered from 1.3B ([E5](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.03533)). GTE trains on about 788M pairs from 33 sources plus 3M fine-tuning pairs ([GTE](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.03281)). zeroshot-v2.0, by contrast, uses 51,731 capped classification texts plus about 885k NLI pairs ([Laurer et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.17543)), and GLiClass pre-trains on 1.2M examples ([GLiClass](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07662)). Contrastive pretraining alone does most of the work: E5's pretrained-only model beats BM25 zero-shot on BEIR without any labelled data (44.2 vs 41.7). A label model built on such an encoder gets its label semantics from pretraining. Classification fine-tuning mostly teaches the matching format, negatives and calibration. It can also wear that geometry down: a bi-encoder fine-tuned on UTCD's nine in-domain datasets scored **9.4% on Banking77** ([UTCD](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16521)).

One number shows where the remaining gap sits. The same frozen bge-base utterance embeddings that score 0.695 against label names reach **0.87 with a logistic-regression probe on 8 examples per intent** ([bge-base card](https://huggingface.co/BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5); [MTEB source](https://github.com/embeddings-benchmark/mteb/blob/1.12.0/mteb/abstasks/AbsTaskClassification.py)). The utterance encoder already separates the intents. What fails is mapping a terse label like "card arrival" onto its region of utterance space. This also means MTEB's Banking77 scores measure 8-shot probes, not zero-shot accuracy.

**The number of distinct training tasks drives transfer, and examples per task saturate early.** In Super-NaturalInstructions, unseen-task performance grows log-linearly up to 757 training tasks, about 64 instances per task is enough, and T5-large with 757 tasks (48.0 ROUGE-L) matches T5-3B with 128 (48.4). Diversity substitutes for size ([Wang et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.07705v3)). In Flan-2022 most of the gain arrives by 282 tasks: the 8B model reaches 20.5 there and only 21.9 at 1,836 ([Chung et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.11416)). T0 adds a second axis: more datasets raise the median, but more prompts per dataset are what cut sensitivity to wording ([Sanh et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.08207)). For classifiers, adding 28 classification datasets (389 classes, capped at 500 texts per class) to NLI training raised held-out zero-shot by 9.4% ([Laurer et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.17543)). GTE's balanced sampling across sources beat size-proportional sampling, 59.0 vs 55.4 ([GTE](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.03281)). No source shows a sharp threshold. The curves are smooth, with diminishing returns beyond a few hundred tasks, and label-reading encoders trained on 9-33 datasets sit on the steep part.

**Domain shift costs about 30 points, and the cost tracks how closely test labels resemble training labels.** On UTCD, models trained on nine in-domain datasets drop sharply on datasets with unseen labels: a BERT cross-encoder falls from 81.9 to 53.2 and a bi-encoder from 80.0 to 44.7. Untrained BART-MNLI scores about the same on both splits (46.1 vs 44.8). So in-domain training raised in-domain accuracy by about 35 points but out-of-domain accuracy by only about 8, and most of what it taught was dataset-specific. UTCD's authors found that transfer correlates with label-token overlap ([UTCD](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.16521)). Held-out macro-F1 for zeroshot-v2.0 shows which label types fail. Binary sentiment transfers almost perfectly (Yelp 0.985). Fine-grained many-class sets do not (Banking77 0.567, MASSIVE 0.518, Manifesto 0.256), and neither do judgements defined by annotation guidelines (HateXplain 0.389) ([zeroshot-v2.0 card](https://huggingface.co/MoritzLaurer/deberta-v3-large-zeroshot-v2.0)).

LIDM's own progression fits this picture. With 152 labels the head memorised. With 2,118 labels the frozen encoder still memorised and LoRA reached 0.434. With 961k labels the head read, but only the label types present. Of all the levers, **the type of label matters most, more than the count**, and the supply of intent-style labels is small. Only about 150 public intents ship official descriptions: SGD's 88 service-intents and NLU++'s 62. Generating descriptions for name-only sets (Redwood without Banking77 and CLINC, the Bitext verticals, MTOP) raises that to several hundred to about 700 fine-grained intents. Much of this pool, such as SGD's bank services, NLU++ banking and Bitext retail banking, sits next to Banking77 and needs separate reporting ([SGD](https://github.com/google-research-datasets/dstc8-schema-guided-dialogue); [NLU++](https://github.com/PolyAI-LDN/task-specific-datasets/tree/master/nlupp); [Redwood](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gxlarson/redwood/main/data/redwood_test.json)).

Synthetic data can stand in for human data when the goal is general label reading, but it does not fix fine-grained intents. zeroshot-v2.0-c, trained on Mixtral texts for 500+ tasks with no human classification data, matches the human-data variant on average (0.676 vs 0.673) and is worse on Banking77 (0.513 vs 0.567) ([zeroshot-v2.0 card](https://huggingface.co/MoritzLaurer/deberta-v3-large-zeroshot-v2.0)). When synthetic data has to replace one task's real training set, the gap is 3-6 macro-F1 on low-subjectivity tasks and about 27 on subjective ones ([Li et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07849v2)).

## Architecture buys a few points; the training signal decides what transfers

Retrieval research gives the cleanest architecture comparison, and it favours training signal over structure. On BEIR, with every model trained on MS MARCO, out-of-domain scores relative to BM25 split as follows: BM25 reranked by a cross-encoder gains **+11%**, ColBERT late interaction +2.5%, the dense bi-encoder TAS-B −2.8%, and DPR −47.7%. In-domain, the neural models sit within about 0.03 of each other ([BEIR](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.08663)). Late interaction's lead follows supervision more than structure. ColBERTv2 is trained to match a cross-encoder's score distribution over hard negatives it retrieves itself, and SPLADEv2, a sparse bi-encoder distilled the same way, ties it over 12 BEIR datasets (**51.9 vs 51.7**), both far above TAS-B's 45.9 ([ColBERTv2](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.01488)). Capacity matters too. A 60M cross-encoder is far above BM25 in-domain but slightly below it out of domain (0.425 vs 0.432), while a 3B one gains +0.10 ([Rosa et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.06121)). LIDM's 33M fusion head sits at the small end of that range. Rerankers also add less over a strong first stage: +6.2 nDCG over BM25 but only +2.2 over a domain-adapted dense retriever ([GPL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.07577)). The best rerankers can get worse when they score deeper candidate lists ([Jacob et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11767)).

Classification results agree. GLiNER's bi-encoder edges out its single-sequence model on CrossNER (61.5 vs 60.9, with different training data) ([GLiNER bi-encoder](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18487)). GLiClass's single-sequence model leads NLI cross-encoders on average (0.719 vs 0.682 F1) and ties them on Banking77 (0.557 vs 0.567) ([GLiClass](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07662)). Both trail modern retrieval bi-encoders, which score 0.63-0.70 there ([BTZSC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11991)). GLiClass also reports that text-label attention weakens as labels grow, and LIDM measured Laya falling from 0.871 to 0.360, so separating label encoding was the right call for high cardinality. What the evidence does not support is the bet that a small fusion head adds back what late interaction loses. In-domain, one or two interaction layers over separate encodings recover all but 1-2 points of a joint encoder's accuracy ([DeFormer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.00697)). The one transfer study, MORES, found that "interaction patterns are more general" while representations need domain data, and adapting only the interaction blocks was its worst variant ([MORES](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.13313)). That is gte-1b's profile exactly. Its matching skill transfers to patent topics, but no interaction layer can rescue encodings that do not know customer intents.

The training signal explains why gte-1's head learned nothing transferable. Adding a trainable head's logits to a fixed scorer's is a product of experts. Gradients push the head toward whatever the fixed expert leaves unexplained, and a learned gate on the fixed expert collapses unless an entropy penalty holds it open ([Clark et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.03683)). Over a strong retrieval prior and a modest label inventory, what remains unexplained is mostly which training labels the prior confuses. That predicts gte-1's 49% per-label offset and its under-weighted prior. Retrieval research has tested countermeasures, though none for label heads on unseen domains. Train against a stronger teacher's score distribution over candidates the prior retrieves, not against the gold label alone: ColBERTv2 uses a KL loss, and GPL uses MarginMSE, which also tolerates false negatives ([GPL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.07577)). Draw negatives from the prior's own top-ranked errors, since CLEAR found random negatives "sharply" reduce accuracy ([CLEAR](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.13969)). Fix the interpolation weight, and select checkpoints, on held-out domains rather than on held-out examples of training labels. LIDM's cross-entropy against a one-hot label over sampled options uses none of these.

Label descriptions follow the same rule: they pay off for readers trained on them. Fine-tuning RoBERTa on a few hundred label-description strings, with no task inputs, raised accuracy by **17-19 points**. Random verbalizers recovered part of that gain, so ablations need a random-description control ([Gao et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.02239)). On unseen CLINC domains, a 3B reader went from 84.3 with names to **90.6 with "dependent" descriptions** written with all sibling intents in view, while descriptions written one intent at a time did nothing (84.2) ([Hong et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sigdial-1.39.pdf)). NLI models that never trained on definitions scored 48.4 with label names and 26.0 with definitions alone ([Yin et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.00161)). Models trained on one schema wording lose 12-18% relative when the descriptions are paraphrased ([SGD-X](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.06800)). LIDM's heads never trained on descriptions, so their inconsistent −5 to +8 response is what this literature predicts. The anti-memorisation devices that work all act on the data: caps per label, negatives drawn from other label sets, class dropout (significant in GoLLIE's ablation), several phrasings per label, and evaluation that holds out whole datasets ([Laurer et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.17543); [GoLLIE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.03668)). One caution: models learn about as fast from irrelevant prompt templates as from instructive ones, so fitting the training data is no evidence that a model reads the label text ([Webson & Pavlick 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01247)).

The published frontier for open models on Banking77 with all labels and no Banking77 training is about 0.70, and LIDM's 0.70 goal sits right on it.

These numbers carry about ±5 points of protocol noise: bge-base scores 0.695 locally and 0.64 in BTZSC. Three traps matter for setting targets. The Jev 0.870 behind LIDM's stretch goal comes from a 72-label, 100-item slice, and on the full test Jev scores 0.792 ([jev-benchmarks](https://github.com/AbdelStark/jev-benchmarks); [DMB](https://github.com/nibzard/decision-model-benchmark)). MTEB's Banking77 scores of about 0.87 are 8-shot probes. And many "zero-shot" models have seen Banking77, because UTCD, tasksource and the training list behind the non-`-c` zeroshot-v2.0 models all include it. The clean Laurer reference is therefore the `-c` model's 0.513 ([tasksource sources](https://huggingface.co/datasets/tasksource/zero-shot-label-nli/raw/main/sources.yaml); [Laurer datasets list](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MoritzLaurer/zeroshot-classifier/main/v1_human_data/datasets_overview.csv)). LIDM's pipeline is clean on Banking77 labels. It still trains on UTCD's CLINC150 split minus its banking intents, so neighbouring intents are partly seen ([stage1.py](../src/cls/data/stage1.py)).

Zero-shot generalization in a new domain has a recognisable shape. Accuracy falls roughly log-linearly with the number of candidates. Bi-encoders lose about 6 points per doubling (bge-base 0.931 at N=5, 0.695 at N=77), while packed readers and LIDM lose 9-12. Granularity matters more than count: open LLMs average 0.807 on 7-label SNIPS, 0.468 on 150-label CLINC150 and 0.388 on 77-label Banking77 ([Ganesh et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27421)). Top-k recall is far better than top-1. gte puts the gold label in its top 5 for 86% of Banking77 questions ([summary.log](../logs/overnight/summary.log)), and a top-10 shortlist over descriptions keeps a 3B reader's full accuracy, 84.88 vs 84.72 ([Hong et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sigdial-1.39.pdf)). Calibration turns accuracy into usable automation: with a validation-fitted threshold, Jev answers 51.2% of the full test at 3.68% error ([DMB](https://github.com/nibzard/decision-model-benchmark)). LIDM's raw calibration is an asset here that no accuracy number captures. So zero-shot gets a new banking domain a strong shortlister and a calibrated cold-start classifier that can confidently automate a share of traffic. It does not reach production-grade top-1 accuracy over 77 confusable intents.

| N=77, `name: description` | 0.732 (bge-base) | 0.73-0.77 | ~0.80 | ≥ 0.85 |
| N=20 (gold + 19 random), names | 0.83-0.84 (bge-base) | 0.85-0.88 | ~0.90 | — |

The names and N=20 rows follow the published evidence. The descriptions row is my inference: the measured bge floor plus the 2-5 points that intent-style pretraining (PIE, +2.5) and training the reader on descriptions (Hong) add on top of descriptions alone. With descriptions, bge already reaches 0.862 at N=20, above GOALS.md's 0.85 ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). No study combines the ingredients below on one fixed small encoder, so their gains overlap and do not add up.

| Ingredient | Evidence | Expected effect from the ~0.70 floor |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptions that contrast neighbouring intents, at test time | bge +3.6 and gte +6.9 locally ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)); +6.3 for a name-trained reader ([Hong et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sigdial-1.39.pdf)); BGE-Large +8.6 across other intent sets ([Hu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.17862)) | +3-7 |

The gap from the ~0.70 floor to full supervision (0.93-0.94) is about 24 points. Descriptions, a distilled reranker and intent-style training together plausibly recover 5-10 of them, to 0.75-0.80. The first 5-10 labelled examples per intent recover 15-18 on their own. The last 6 points need tens to hundreds of examples per intent. LIDM is not on this ladder yet. On intents its best head trails its own retrieval term by 18 points (0.456 vs 0.634), and a component that loses to the prior cannot add to it.

## Conclusion

The experiments show what LIDM's head actually is: a reader bound to its training schemas. It beats retrieval by about 20 points where labels resemble its training labels and loses by 16-20 where they do not. That, with cached-option latency and good raw calibration, makes it a credible scorer for a fixed or slowly changing schema with some in-domain label text to train on. It is not the cold-start reader of new intent schemas that GOALS.md set out to build, and for that use case the kill criterion's spirit already applies. The gap is on the label side. The same frozen bge utterance embeddings go from 0.695 to 0.87 once eight examples per intent define each label's region, and every zero-shot lever that works (descriptions, generated utterances, prototypes) improves how labels are represented, not how deeply they interact with the text. The next investment belongs in label representation and training signal, not deeper fusion.

One experiment would settle LIDM's zero-shot case. Rerank bge's or gte's top 10-20 using `name: description` options. Train the reranker against a strong teacher's score distribution on intent-style, non-banking data, with negatives mined from the prior, and fix the retrieval weight on held-out domains. Report results stratified by how close each test label is to the training labels, against the **0.732** bge-with-descriptions floor rather than the 0.695 names floor. If the reranker cannot clear 0.732 by more than the ±1.6-point noise band, the head adds nothing for unseen intents. The targets should be restated accordingly. About 0.72 with names and 0.75-0.77 with descriptions are achievable. 0.79, Jev's full-test score rather than its 72-label 0.87, is the stretch. Anything above 0.85 is a few-shot problem.
