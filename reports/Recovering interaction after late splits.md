# Move option-state interaction into the backbone

The architectures with measured evidence of recovering a cross-encoder's token-level interaction all let the candidate meet the context inside pretrained backbone layers. None of them does it in a head added after the encoder. Two families come close. Deferred-joint encoders lose only 1-2 points on RACE and MNLI when the top quarter of BERT-base runs jointly and is distilled from the full model. Masked packed encoders such as Verdict-MQ, on ModernBERT-large, come within 1.5 RACE points of a matched baseline and score above Laya on typed decisions (0.801 vs 0.767). Multi-vector late interaction closes only about a quarter of an NLI gap and scores below random on negation pairs. The best matching heads add at most 1-2 points on strong encoders. LIDM sits at the extreme of the decomposed family, with zero joint backbone layers. That is where every published split-depth curve breaks: SQuAD 2.0 loses 25 F1 with 1 of 12 layers joint. LIDM's head already applies the head-level lessons (it keeps option tokens and copies ModernBERT's top layers), and extra head depth and looping changed nothing in LIDM's own runs. What is left to change is *where* options meet the state. The recommendation is **LIDM-X**: every option token attends to pass A's keys and values at every layer, options are isolated from each other, and they share position IDs. Mathematically this is one masked packed pass. It adds no parameters, keeps the no-budget property behind the +11 on long label descriptions, and costs about 9% more attention FLOPs. The alternative, **LIDM-DJ**, runs the top 7 of 28 layers jointly for each option, so the state can also read the option, at roughly 1.7-2x Laya's token cost. A stage-2 retrain of each candidate, compared with the existing LIDM and Laya checkpoints (about 2-3 h per run on the 4070 Super), decides between them. Realistically, expect LIDM-X to recover half to two-thirds of the VitaminC and RACE gaps and some of the short-label gap. Cascades do not pay while LIDM is slower than Laya.

## LIDM sits where every split-depth curve breaks

The cleanest evidence comes from studies that move the split layer on one backbone and one task. The Decoupled Transformer sweep on ELECTRA-base is the main example. It runs the lower layers separately per passage and the upper layers jointly. SQuAD 2.0 F1 goes from **87.6 (full joint) to 86.7 with 7 of 12 layers joint, 84.0 with 3, 80.6 with 2 and 62.6 with 1** ([Decoupled Transformer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.02765)). DeFormer (BERT-base, 3 of 12 layers joint, with distillation) loses **1.8 RACE points (66.3 to 64.5)**, 1.0 on BoolQ and 1.8 on MNLI. Its authors report that the loss is negligible when only lower layers are separated and "jumps sharply" when the last layers are ([DeFormer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.00697)). PreTTR shows the same cliff on ranking: with 1 joint layer, Robust04 nDCG@20 falls from 0.436 to 0.259, below BM25 ([PreTTR](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.14255)). LIDM keeps all 28 ModernBERT layers separate and lets options interact only in a 2-block head. It is one step beyond the right-hand end of all three curves.

The curves also explain *which* tasks LIDM loses. Relevance ranking survives very shallow interaction: two MORES interaction blocks reach **98% of a BERT cross-encoder's MRR@10** (0.3456 vs 0.3527) ([MORES](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.13313)). One DC-BERT interaction layer keeps 97.7% of NQ-Open P@10 ([DC-BERT](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.12591)). Fine-grained pair tasks do not: even with 7 of 12 layers joint, the Decoupled Transformer loses **2.2 points on MNLI and 9.4 on MRPC** ([Decoupled Transformer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.02765)). LIDM's results split the same way. It ties Laya on typed decisions (0.769 vs 0.757 after stage 2 + 2c). It loses 7 points on VitaminC and 4.7 on RACE, where one changed fact must be checked against the option ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). Scale offers some hope: LUMEN reports that the live (joint) fraction needed to match Fusion-in-Decoder *shrinks* with model size, with 1/3 sufficient at T5-XXL ([LUMEN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.10448)). A 395M encoder sits at the small end, so the 12-layer fractions (a quarter to a half joint) are the better guide. That translation is an extrapolation: no study measured a split curve on ModernBERT, VitaminC, RACE-style zero-shot reading or Banking77.

Before choosing a fix, it matters what LIDM's head already does. Its fusion blocks keep every option token rather than a pooled vector. All options of a question self-attend, then cross-attend to pass-A tokens, and each option is scored at its `[MASK]` marker. The blocks are initialised by copying ModernBERT's top two layers ([head.py](../src/cls/head.py), [model.py](../src/cls/model.py)). That already applies MORES's two cheapest lessons. Copying top-layer weights is the one MORES found decisive: random initialisation dropped MRR@10 from 0.3456 to 0.2723 ([MORES](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2004.13313)). More head depth has also been tried without effect. Eight unshared blocks, or looping two blocks up to eight times, did not beat the plain two-block head on the M1 mixture. Looping added nothing on Banking77 after stage 1 ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). This matches the literature: DUMA's co-attention head is best at 2 layers and drops slightly with more ([DUMA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.09415)). A 12-layer model looped twice still trails a true 24-layer model on open-book QA (30.8 vs 33.9) ([Saunshi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.17416)). So what separates LIDM from a joint encoder is not head capacity. Its options spend 28 layers blind to the state, and its state never sees an option.

## Only interaction inside pretrained layers closes most of the gap

The five families differ in where the option first meets the context, and that predicts how much of the cross-encoder's accuracy they keep.

### Late-interaction scorers fail exactly where VitaminC bites

Multi-vector late interaction does well on ranking and badly on entailment. On response selection, poly-encoders close 30-90% of the bi-to-cross gap at near-bi-encoder latency (23 ms vs 2.6 s for 1,000 candidates) ([Poly-encoder](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.01969)). On MNLI the picture changes: **cross-encoder 83.7, dual encoder 75.2, Poly-360 77.3, MixEncoder 77.5-78.4**, so pooled-candidate designs recover 25-38% of the gap ([MixEncoder](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.05261)). VIRT, which lets token states cross-attend in the last layer and distils attention maps, still trails by 5.5 points (78.6 vs 84.1) ([VIRT](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.04195)). Token-level MaxSim does better on retrieval: under the same teacher, ColBERT closes about 70% of the dual-to-cross gap on MS MARCO (0.383 vs 0.355 and 0.395), and a learned scorer over the similarity matrix (LITE) closes 95% ([LITE](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.17968v1)). A 2026 same-backbone reproduction is less kind. There, ColBERT fell **6.8 nDCG points** below its cross-encoder, while the mid-fusion designs MORES and PreTTR stayed within 1.5 ([MICE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16299)).

The decisive problem is negation and single-token flips. On NevIR, where two documents differ only by negation, ColBERTv1 and v2 reach **19.7% and 13.0% paired accuracy against a 25% random baseline**, while cross-encoders reach up to 50.6%. The authors trace this to MaxSim never selecting negation tokens as the maximum for any query token ([NevIR](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.07614v2)). A SIGIR 2025 reproduction found that only cross-encoders generalise across negation benchmarks ([van den Elsen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13506v3)). A sum of maxima also cannot *penalise* a contradicting token, so one mismatch is outvoted by many matches. LIDM's learned cross-attention over option tokens is already more expressive than MaxSim. Swapping it for ColBERT-style scoring would move LIDM backwards on VitaminC. Storage, the main concern of this literature, is irrelevant here: all 77 Banking77 labels fit in well under 1 MB of token vectors.

### Better heads narrow the gap by a point or two, not five

Matching heads on strong encoders give small gains. On ALBERT-xxlarge and ELECTRA-large, OCN, WAE and BiDAF add 0.2-0.9 points on RACE or DREAM. Only DUMA's bidirectional co-attention still gives **+1.4 on RACE (86.6 to 88.0)** and +1.6 to +2.0 on DREAM ([DUMA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.09415)). DCMN+ is the head closest to LIDM because it encodes passage, question and option separately. It beats a joint `[CLS]` baseline by 2.7-3.8 RACE points on BERT and XLNet ([DCMN+](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1908.11511)). Re-implemented on ALBERT-xxlarge and ELECTRA-large, it falls **0.9-1.2 points below** the joint baseline ([DUMA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.09415)). A clever head on separately encoded options does not catch a joint strong encoder. DUMA's direction ablation is the one transferable signal: both attention directions give +2.84 on DREAM against +2.06 for the better single direction. LIDM has only options-read-state. For fact verification, KGAT's kernel-pooled token-similarity features beat plain dot-product attention by 0.65-1.0 FEVER-score points ([KGAT](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.09796)). That is a cheap feature to add, but a small one.

The large VitaminC lever is data, not architecture. On the same ALBERT-base cross-encoder, training on VitaminC's contrastive pairs instead of FEVER lifts VitaminC-real from **54.8 to 86.2** and the contrast-flip rate from 55.5% to 84.1% ([VitaminC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.08541)). Counterfactually augmented SNLI does the same at small scale: 8.3k mixed examples beat 500k original ones on revised premises (64.6 vs 54.3) ([Kaushik et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.12434)). That lifts both LIDM and Laya, so it does not close the *relative* gap. The ANLI and CondaQA floors are an encoder and data problem, not a split problem: fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-large sits at 57.1 on CondaQA against a 47.8 majority baseline ([CondaQA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.00295)). Neither candidate architecture should be judged on those two tasks.

## Masked packing turns LIDM's two passes into one joint computation

Packed encoders put the context and every candidate into one sequence. A structured mask decides who sees whom, and three independent results agree on the rules. Uni-Encoder (BERT-base, Ubuntu V2, 10 candidates) blocks candidate-to-candidate attention and gives all candidates the same position IDs. It reaches **0.859 R10@1 against 0.844 for a per-pair cross-encoder** in the authors' controlled ablation, at about 4x the speed ([Uni-Encoder](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2106.01263)). Unique position IDs cost 2.2 points, and letting candidates attend to each other costs 3.3. UniMC finds the mask carries almost everything: 78.0 average accuracy with the isolation mask alone, against 39.1 with position-ID changes alone ([UniMC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.08590)). Verdict-MQ, on LIDM's own backbone, reports that options which can see each other "collapse to a single representation" and the model stops learning ([Verdict-MQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20778)).

Verdict-MQ is the closest published relative of LIDM, and the most important single source here. It is a single, unreplicated October 2026 preprint. Its mask has four rules. The state attends only to itself. Each question attends to the state and itself. Each option attends to the state, its question and itself. Options share position IDs, with ModernBERT's sliding window defined on position IDs rather than sequence index. On the 400-case typed-decisions test it scores **0.801 ± 0.005 against 0.767 for Laya** (KL 0.080 vs 0.117), with ModernBERT-large ([Verdict-MQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20778)). That Laya figure equals LIDM's own measurement of the published Laya-TD checkpoint on the same 400 cases ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)), which suggests the benchmarks match. The +3.4 cannot be credited to the mask alone, because Verdict's large model starts from a tasksource NLI checkpoint. On fine-tuned benchmarks it scores **77.4 on RACE against 78.9** for an under-specified matched baseline, and 76.2 ± 3.1 against 81.0 on an unstable BoolQ. Packing all questions of a case into one pass makes it 1.8x faster than per-question passes at batch 1 and 2.8x at batch 8. Verdict's "question-aware state copies" give each question its own copy of the state, which is what LIDM's pass A already does. Those copies add **+1.2 on RACE and +2.2 on MultiRC** at 1.4-2.5x latency and nothing on typed decisions. So LIDM's per-question state re-read, the cause of its latency deficit, buys accuracy on reading tasks.

The key structural fact is an equivalence. Once the state attends only to itself, the state's keys and values at every layer do not depend on the options. A packed pass is then *exactly* a state pass plus an option pass, in which each option's attention at layer ℓ reads the concatenation of the state's layer-ℓ keys and values and its own. That is LIDM's pass A and pass B, with one change: pass B sees pass A at every layer instead of only in the head. Uni-Encoder's arrow mask, where the context also sees candidates, lacks this property. The same formulation avoids the main 12 GB risk of a custom packed mask. A non-block-diagonal mask cannot use Flash-Attention's variable-length kernel, and a fallback to the math kernel at 4,096 tokens materialises about 0.5 GB of scores per layer. An option pass attending to cached state keys needs only (S + O)·O scores per option and runs on plain batched SDPA.

Packing alone does not fix many short labels. GLiClass packs labels with the text (without an isolation mask). Its throughput stays nearly flat from 1 to 128 labels (46.3 to 32.9 examples/s for the ModernBERT-large variant), while an NLI cross-encoder falls from 16.8 to 0.25. Yet on Banking77 it only ties the per-label cross-encoder (0.557 vs 0.567). Its authors report that label tokens crowd out the text as label counts grow, and that ModernBERT variants trail DeBERTa-v3 by about 10 points on average ([GLiClass](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07662)). Two ModernBERT details matter for any reimplementation. Two of every three layers use a 128-token local window, so the window must be computed on position IDs; otherwise options either leak into each other or lose sight of the state ([ModernBERT](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.13663)). The mask must also be present during training: Block-Attention's block-diagonal Llama3-8B falls from 67.9% to 48.0% without fine-tuning and returns to 68.4% after a few hundred to a thousand steps ([Block-Attention](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.15355v4)).

## Cascades cannot pay while LIDM is slower than Laya; distillation can

Cascades work when the cheap stage is cheap and its confidence is honest. In Model Cascading, a BERT-medium to BERT-base cascade matches BERT-base on MNLI at **5.26 vs 10.19 GFLOPs, escalating 22% of inputs** on calibrated MaxProb ([Model Cascading](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05528)). Tabi answers 50-70% of queries with its first model ([Tabi](https://cse.hkust.edu.hk/~kaichen/papers/tabi-eurosys23.pdf)). Cascading complete models beats exiting from shallow internal layers: at about 3x speed-up, CascadeBERT keeps 81.2 MNLI-m against DeeBERT's 63.2 ([CascadeBERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14682)). LIDM has the right confidence signal (ECE 0.041 after temperature scaling) but the wrong cost profile: **64 ms per 5-question case against Laya's 44** ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). A LIDM-to-Laya cascade is slower than running Laya alone, and on escalated items it can at best match Laya. LIDM is the better first stage only with many long options, and there it is also the better model. That is a static switch on option profile, not a cascade. A shortlist-then-joint-rerank design remains a fallback for large label sets. Du et al. found joint selection on Banking77 peaks at **k = 25 shortlisted labels**, and seeing competing options in the joint pass beats pairwise scoring by about 2.5-3.0 points ([Du et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.00301)). That design reaches for Laya rather than repairing LIDM.

Distillation from the joint model is the one cascade-family lever that transfers directly, because Laya already exists on the same backbone and data. DeFormer's output distillation plus layer-wise representation matching is worth **+1.3 F1 on BERT-base and +3.3 on BERT-large** ([DeFormer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.00697)). Dropping KL distillation from the Decoupled Transformer costs 2.7 F1 ([Decoupled Transformer](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2108.02765)). Distillation helps most when the student keeps token-level interaction. With Margin-MSE, ColBERT closes 68% of its gap to the teacher and a decomposed PreTT student exceeds it (0.378 vs 0.376), while a single-vector BERT-dot gains almost nothing (0.320 to 0.321) ([Margin-MSE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2010.02666)). A shared-weight model trained in both modes can even improve its joint mode: 54.7 vs 52.6 MSCOCO image R@1 against a separately trained cross-encoder ([Geigle et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.11920)). Distillation does not lift the architectural ceiling: under one teacher, ColBERT still stays at 0.383 against 0.395 ([LITE](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.17968v1)). It belongs on top of the architecture change, not in place of it.

The compute picture for the candidates follows from token-layer counts. Up to a few thousand tokens, ModernBERT-large cost is dominated by its linear layers, so cost scales with tokens × layers. Let S be state+question tokens, N the number of options and ΣO the total option tokens. Laya, current LIDM and LIDM-X all cost about 28·(S + ΣO). LIDM-X adds only the attention from option tokens to S extra keys, about 4·S·d FLOPs per token per layer, or roughly 9% at S = 512. Re-encoding the state with each option in the top k layers (LIDM-DJ) adds k·(N−1)·S. At k = 7 that is about **+67% over Laya on RACE (S ≈ 400, N = 4) and +98% on Banking77 names (S ≈ 25, N = 77)**. Those token counts are rough assumptions, and batch-1 latency on a 4070 Super is not linear in tokens, so both must be measured. Memory is not the binding constraint. Pass A's keys and values for all 28 layers take about 115 KB per token, about 59 MB for a 512-token state. Verdict trained ModernBERT-large packed cases at batch 4 on a 16 GB laptop GPU ([Verdict-MQ](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20778)). Laya's stage 2 (max length 1,024) and LIDM's LoRA stage 1 (5.5-6.6 GB peak) already train on this card ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). LIDM-X's training graph is about the size of one Laya joint pass. LIDM-DJ's grows with N copies of the state in the top layers and needs small micro-batches for 77-option items. Gradient checkpointing is already enabled.

## LIDM-X first, LIDM-DJ as the test of direction

**LIDM-X (per-layer option-to-state attention).** Pass A is unchanged: `[CLS] state [SEP] question [SEP]`, run once per question. That keeps the question-aware state Verdict found worth 1-2 points on reading tasks. Pass B becomes a custom loop over ModernBERT's 28 layers that reuses each layer's own weights, with LoRA applied as today. At every layer, each option token's queries attend to [pass-A keys and values at that layer ; its own option's keys and values]. Pass-A keys and values can be recomputed from the per-layer hidden states pass A already returns (check whether the last returned state is taken before or after the final norm) or captured with hooks. Option positions start at len(pass A) for every option, so options share position IDs. Options never attend to each other. In local layers, the window uses position distance, so options see the last ~64 positions of pass A (where the question sits); global layers see all of it. The existing 2-block head and retrieval prior stay on top, unlooped, so option-to-option comparison still happens once, in the head. The design is Verdict-MQ's rule 3 with full-depth question-aware state and without Verdict's question-sees-options rule. It has no new parameters, no option-token budget and exact permutation invariance in the backbone. It gives up caching option encodings for fixed label sets, which the current code does not exploit anyway: it re-encodes options per batch because caching all layers would not fit in VRAM ([model.py](../src/cls/model.py)).

**LIDM-DJ (deferred joint top layers, DeFormer pattern).** Layers 1-21 run exactly as LIDM does now: pass A and each option alone, with lower-layer option states cacheable. Layers 22-28 run jointly on [pass A ; option i], one copy per option, with positions continuing after pass A and options still isolated from each other. The option marker is then scored as before. Seven of 28 layers matches DeFormer's and the Decoupled Transformer's quarter-depth joint fraction. LIDM-DJ is the only candidate in which the state reads each option. That is the direction LUMEN found most damaging to remove ([LUMEN](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.10448)) and DUMA found additive (+2.84 vs +2.06) ([DUMA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.09415)). Its price is the k·(N−1)·S duplication.

**The deciding experiment.** Train LIDM-X and LIDM-DJ from the same stage-1 LoRA checkpoint, with the same single pass over the stage-2 mix, the same learning rate (1e-4) and the same hard labels. That way the existing stage-2 LIDM and Laya checkpoints serve as the reference arms without retraining. At the stage-2 run's 2 h 12 min, each new arm should take about 2.5-4 h on the 4070 Super, and each run needs a hard time limit. Then run the same evaluations as today. The context suite covers VitaminC and RACE (n = 1,000, about ±3 points each). The label suite covers Banking77 names and descriptions and the 13-40-option slice (n = 217, about ±6). Add typed decisions after 2c with the per-type temperature step, latency per 5-question case at batch 1, and peak VRAM. Use paired bootstrap CIs, as already done ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)). The primary metric is the mean LIDM − Laya difference on the four rows LIDM loses today (VitaminC −7, RACE −4.7, Banking77 names −11.3, 13-40 options −9.2), currently **−8.1**. The guardrails are that Banking77 descriptions stay at least +5 over Laya and typed decisions stay within 1.5 points of Laya.

Adopt LIDM-X if it halves the primary gap (to about −4 or better) and LIDM-DJ does not beat it by 2 or more points averaged over VitaminC and RACE. In that case one-way, every-layer access is enough. Adopt LIDM-DJ if it does beat LIDM-X by that margin and its latency stays within about 1.5x Laya's. If neither halves the gap, the late split is not the main cause, which would contradict the same-encoder Laya evidence. The fallback is then a static switch: Laya for short option sets, LIDM for many long ones. Afterwards, add Laya-teacher distillation (KL over option distributions, backlog item 1) to the winner only. Optional ablations inside the LIDM-X arm can follow if time allows. One masks the head's option-to-option attention: Uni-Encoder found cross-candidate attention harmful, while Du et al. found seeing competitors helpful. The other starts option-state attention at layer 14 rather than layer 1, which MICE found near-lossless on 12-layer rerankers and which restores lower-layer option caching ([MICE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16299)).

The Banking77-names rows are the least certain. bge alone scores 0.625 there against Laya's 0.547 ([RESULTS.md](../docs/RESULTS.md)), so the retrieval prior is likely a bigger lever for short label sets than either architecture. Laya's state *does* see all options, so neither candidate fully reproduces what Laya does on short lists. Single-seed differences under about 3 points on any one context-suite task are noise.

## Conclusion

The late split has been framed as a head-design problem, and the evidence says it is a layer-placement problem. LIDM already has the head the literature recommends, and more of it did not help. Every design that closes most of the gap gives the option access to the context inside pretrained layers. Because the state can be made independent of the options, "two passes plus a head" and "one masked joint pass" turn out to be the same computation. LIDM and Laya then differ only in their masks: Laya lets the state and options see everything, while LIDM-X lets options see the state at every layer and nothing else. LIDM's distinctive advantages survive that change. The absence of an option budget comes from isolation and shared positions, not from separate encoding. Calibration after temperature scaling is a training property, not an architectural one.

The experiment's most informative outcome would be the LIDM-X vs LIDM-DJ difference on VitaminC. A small difference means option-reads-state is sufficient. That keeps LIDM's cost profile and leaves room for a state-once, Verdict-style layout to beat Laya's latency later. A large one means the state must be re-read per option, and the split's speed argument largely disappears for short-state tasks.
